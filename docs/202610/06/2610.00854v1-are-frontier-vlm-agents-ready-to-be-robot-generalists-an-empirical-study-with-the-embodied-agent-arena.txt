Are Frontier VLM Agents
Ready to Be Robot Generalists?
An Empirical Study with the Embodied Agent Arena
Haojian Huang1,3, Pukun Zhao3, Zexi Li2,3, Yehang Zhang1,3, Yangkai Wei3,
Wenqian Li3, Han Yang3, Kaiwen Zhou3, Ying-Cong Chen1,3,†, Yinchuan Li3,†
1 HKUST (Guangzhou)
2 The Chinese University of Hong Kong
3 Knowin AI
† Corresponding authors
Abstract. Frontier vision-language models (VLMs) combine scene estimation,
interaction grounding, and executable actions. Understanding how these abilities
support complete robotic tasks is central to evaluating their readiness as robot
generalists. We introduce Embodied Agent Arena to examine where local compe-
tence supports, or falls short of, complete task success across Geometry, Spatial
Reasoning, Affordance, Task Planning, and Manipulation. The arena contains
1,000 cases drawn from 32 established sources and GeoProbe, our new benchmark
for geometric estimation on Blender renders and real-scene images. A minimal
harness preserves source observations and operations while separating metric
precision, functional grounding, and native goal completion. We evaluate seven
VLMs, analyze Astra’s task-specific advantages, and compare richer-observation
execution protocols and multi-round review. Across the arena, Astra’s advantage
is strongest in precise estimation and usable-contact localization; completing
coordinated, goal-directed actions remains the key gap to robot generalism.

1 Introduction
A robot generalist must turn scene understanding into actions that satisfy a complete goal.
Estimating where an object lies, selecting a usable contact, and executing a grasp are useful
abilities, yet none establishes that the object reaches its required destination. Vision-language
models (VLMs) increasingly address spatial relations (Ma et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2024) and
action-relevant grounding (Wang et al., 2025a, Song et al., 2024). Learned visuomotor policies
connect observations to robot actions (Kim et al., 2024, Black et al., 2024), while language-
model controllers select skills or generate executable programs (Ahn et al., 2022, Liang et al.,
2022). Frontier VLM agents now combine observation, programming, and feedback-driven
decisions (Fu et al., 2026, Guo et al., 2026), making the relationship between local competence
and complete execution a practical evaluation question.
Capability breadth and reliable completion require different evidence. An average score can
hide whether errors concern metric scale, an object’s reference frame, or the final condition
of a placement. Conversely, a terminal failure can conceal useful progress. Evaluating these
outcomes separately reveals what a model can do and which additional requirements remain
unsatisfied. Generalist readiness therefore calls for both cross-capability comparisons and

We ask: Are frontier VLM agents ready to be robot generalists? Embodied Agent Arena eval-
uates the same models across Geometry, Spatial Reasoning, Affordance, Task Planning, and
Manipulation (Figure 1). Its 1,000 cases combine 32 established sources with GeoProbe, our
geometric-estimation benchmark. GeoProbe varies camera motion, object motion, and scale
references to test which quantities remain accurately estimated as visual conditions change.
A minimal harness preserves source observations and operations. Continuous errors, task-
specific evidence, and terminal success reveal complementary aspects of agent capability.
Our comparisons locate the remaining gap in task requirements: Astra produces the closest
paths yet often misses their endpoints, and its local manipulation strengths coexist with
low household-goal completion. Under matched budgets and execution limits, richer visual
observations reverse Astra’s CALVIN lead while producing gains on other tasks. A separate
multi-round review study measures which answers improve after reinspection or specialist
measurements. Our contributions are:
• An evaluation perspective centered on task requirements. We distinguish local estimates
and interaction evidence from complete goal satisfaction across five robotic capability
domains.
• A unified arena with a new geometric benchmark, GeoProbe. We combine 32 established
sources with 168 GeoProbe cases, using controlled Blender scenes and real images to
diagnose camera, motion, depth, and scale estimation.

2 Related Work
Embodied evaluation has developed along two complementary directions. Control suites
measure execution and task generalization through robot interfaces (James et al., 2019, Mees
et al., 2021), whereas visual diagnostics isolate spatial inference (Ma et al., 2024, Yang et al.,
2024) and interaction grounding (Song et al., 2024, Qian & Fouhey, 2023). The former es-
tablishes whether a goal is achieved; the latter resolves particular perceptual or reasoning
requirements. Concurrently, Hard Vision, Easy Vision (Rasheed et al., 2026) maps frontier
models against specialist and human references across general vision, including geometry,
grounding, and online navigation. Its visual-capability perspective complements our focus on
action-conditioned contacts, robot control, and complete task goals.
Broader evaluations connect perception with decision-making (Li et al., 2024b, Qi et al., 2025),
including navigation and object interaction through executable interfaces (Yang et al., 2025a,
Cheng et al., 2025). Embodied Arena combines fixed probes and interaction (Ni et al., 2025).
Interfaces also change the model’s work: code supports reusable controllers (Fu et al., 2026),
while persistent state introduces memory demands (Zhang et al., 2026). Embodied Agent Arena
connects visual diagnostics with robot execution by separately scoring geometric estimates,
contact grounding, and terminal outcomes (Table 1), then examining how execution settings
change model rankings.
3 The Embodied Agent Arena
3.1 Benchmark Construction and Capability Domains
Data construction. Embodied Agent Arena contains 1,000 cases from 32 established sources
and one new benchmark, GeoProbe, organized into five capability domains (Figures 1–2). Each
case specifies observations, a query or goal, operations, and scoring. We introduce GeoProbe
to diagnose the metric ambiguities behind geometric estimates. Its 168 cases vary camera
motion, object motion, and scale references in controlled Blender scenes, complemented by
real images. For interactive cases, the recorded terminal state connects the agent’s action
sequence to the task’s goal conditions. Appendix A gives source allocation and adaptations.
Geometry. Given images, the agent estimates intrinsics, pose, depth, dimensions, or displace-
ment. InFlux and Map-free test calibration and relative pose (Liang et al., 2025, Arnold et al.,
2022); GeoProbe varies camera motion and scale references. TraceSpatial-Bench adds paths
on 56 scenes (Zhou et al., 2026), testing reference-trajectory error alongside start, arrival, and
collision constraints. These targets separate numerical precision from geometric feasibility.
Spatial Reasoning. The agent infers relations, viewpoints, configurations, and temporal order
from images or video (Ma et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025b, Yang et al., 2024). Examples include
comparing object distances, imagining another observer’s view, and inferring camera motion.
Each question specifies the reference frame and answer format: a category, quantity, or point
set.

Manipulation. The agent executes robot motions or skills for placement, insertion, articulation,
and coordinated handling (James et al., 2019, Chen et al., 2025a). Language-conditioned variants

use the resulting observations and feedback. Variables persist across Python rounds, and
images produced during a round enter the next model request. Appendix B.1 specifies these
interfaces; Section 4.4 examines multi-round review with specialist visual tools (Zhang et al.,

3.3 Evaluation Protocol and Metrics
①Task success. Spatial Reasoning and Planning report Pass@1. Manipulation reports native
success, consecutive goals completed, and task-specific condition or control-evidence scores.
Categorical spatial accuracy is reported separately. ②Geometric precision. Geometry and
numerical spatial queries use MAE, RMSE, AbsRel/MAPE, displacement error, and pose errors
according to the target. Tracing pairs trajectory RMSE with offline 3D constraint satisfaction.
③Native affordance grounding. Predicted boxes use GT-mask IoU, with contact validity scored
separately. Box–box IoU and strict box success at IoU≥0.5 measure localization using the
same rectangular representation. ④Variation and resource use. Repeated runs report the
mean and standard deviation (SD) across runs; SD measures run-to-run variation. Tokens
measure inference use; action calls measure executed control. Section 4.1 specifies repetitions;
Appendix B.3 defines aggregation.

4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
①Models and configuration. We compare five closed-source VLMs (Astra, Sol, Fable, Qwen-
Max, Gemini) and two open-source VLMs (Qwen-397B, Qwen-27B) under the interfaces in
Section 3. Budgets are source-specific and matched between Baseline and RGB-expanded;
Appendix B.2 records versions and limits. ②Evaluation and aggregation. Fixed-input tasks are
assigned five runs per case, or three for Fable; tracing and interactive tasks use one adopted
run. Manipulation covers 182 binary tasks under Baseline, with an RGB-expanded comparison
in Section 4.4. Binary rates retain assigned cases; continuous errors use valid estimates.
Appendix B.4 specifies coverage and repetition accounting.
4.2 Main Results
Astra’s advantage spans precision, grounding, and execution. In Table 2, Astra has the lowest
pose errors (17.1◦, 106.6 cm), the highest contact validity (60.3%), and the highest Planning
success (80.3%). Its baseline Manipulation success is 41.8%, versus Fable’s 24.7%. Figure 3
compares the resulting capability profiles using the fixed transformations in Appendix C.3.
The leading model depends on what success requires. Astra’s Spatial Pass@1 narrowly
exceeds Qwen-Max’s (69.4% versus 68.9%), but Gemini has lower numerical AbsRel (0.161
versus 0.213). Astra’s mask IoU is also close to Fable’s (0.286 versus 0.284). The task mixture
matters: excluding ScienceWorld and VirtualHome lowers Astra’s Planning score to 66.7%, still
above Fable’s 33.3% (Appendix C.2). The Planning lead survives this change in task composition;
in Spatial Reasoning, task success and numerical precision favor different models.

Qwen-Max’s 96%, showing that contact localization and region extent are distinct even under

and 26.7%; the RAGNet counterparts reach 25% and 20%, below 31.7% and 50% (Figure 6c).
The same panel shows zero success on both ReasonAff free-movement categories despite
nonzero RAGNet scores. Contact choice therefore depends on the action–scene pairing, beyond
recognizing a tool’s functional part.

5 Conclusion
Findings. The gap to robot generalism lies in converting local competence into complete
goal satisfaction. Astra’s strongest advantages span geometric estimation, usable-contact
localization, and compound household planning. Richer visual observations also change
control rankings under matched execution limits. Across the arena, success depends on
satisfying the task’s coupled requirements: geometric boundaries, object relations, and final
physical states. These requirements connect local progress to complete outcomes.
Limitations. The arena samples five mainstream robotic capability domains through
bounded tasks and source-specific budgets. Tasks exceeding two hours, substantially larger
budgets, and broader interactive repetition remain to be studied. Public task assets leave
unseen-environment generalization open, while source-specific helpers vary the balance be-
tween skill selection and low-level control. Physical-robot transfer and sustained recovery
from accumulated errors require further evaluation.
Future work. Future studies should vary observations and budgets independently, extend
evaluation to physical robots and multi-hour tasks, and compare specialized controllers.
Tracking error accumulation, recovery, and preservation of achieved conditions will reveal
whether local progress persists across actions and changing scene configurations. The central
test is whether agents can coordinate these capabilities and reliably complete robotic goals
over extended operation.

B.3 Scoring and Repeated Evaluation
Submission and termination. A final submission commits a fixed-input prediction. Interactive
runs end when the agent stops, the environment reaches a terminal condition, or a source-
specific round, token, action, or time limit is reached. The evaluator scores the submitted
output or recorded state independently of the agent’s declaration. A complete episode includes
all its internal reasoning, actions, and recovery; these steps are not separate attempts.
Task scores and aggregation. Categorical questions use answer accuracy and interactive tasks
use native goal success. Invalid or absent predictions remain in success denominators. Invalid
reference annotations are excluded before scoring. Continuous errors pool valid predictions
with compatible metrics and units, alongside their coverage. Numerical Spatial Reasoning uses
exact-match success, |ˆy −y| < 10−6, alongside continuous errors; this differs from VSI-Bench’s
official mean relative accuracy. Point-set questions retain their local set criterion.

Offline 3D success requires valid initiation, target arrival, and collision checks to pass together.
The predicted start must lie within 20 cm of the object point cloud or the reference start. At
least one of the final three predicted points must lie within 20 cm of the target 3D box; for
two-point paths, only the endpoint is checked. Translating the object point cloud to each
checked predicted position must yield at most 20% overlap with the environment. Collision is
checked at the sampled positions. Missing or invalid trajectories count as failures. 2D tracing
reports continuous error. Qwen-27B trajectory outputs undergo format normalization that
preserves all predicted coordinate values.
Native affordance grounding. For a directly predicted bounding box B and ground-truth

separate diagnostic uses IoUbox = |B ∩BG|/|B ∪BG| against the reference box BG. Strict box
success requires IoUbox ≥0.5; contact validity is scored independently. The source field named
giou contains ordinary box–mask IoU, not generalized IoU.

B.4 Repetition Protocol and Result Accounting
Repetitions. Non-tracing Geometry, Spatial Reasoning, and Affordance use five repetitions
per case for six models and three for Fable. Spatial tracing, Planning, and Manipulation have
one reported episode per assignment and therefore no run-to-run SD. Manipulation’s two
configurations appear in Appendix E.1.
Additional execution configurations. Fixed-input evaluation also uses per-case limits of up
to 48 code rounds and 480k tokens for Geometry and Affordance, 24 rounds and 384k tokens
for non-VSI Spatial Reasoning, and 32 rounds and 960k tokens for VSI. Task time limits reach
7,200 seconds, or 10,800 seconds for VSI. Budgets vary by source and execution configuration.
Result selection. Fixed-input and Planning trial slots use the first valid outcome, independent
of correctness. Manipulation uses the fixed adopted baseline and RGB-expanded manifests.

Observation and interaction settings. Baseline and RGB-expanded share the per-source

current scene’s RGB views at every model decision. VIMA also supplies task reference images.

use the same agent loop: one code response may execute several actions before the next model
decision.

ten ordinary groups use temperature zero, with neither explicit reasoning effort nor a separate
thinking budget. Of these, 7,372 request 3,600 response tokens; six use smaller limits as the
case budget runs low. The configurations use a 3,600-token response ceiling and a 12-minute
request timeout. Reported reasoning usage is recorded separately and counted once in the case
total, avoiding duplication when included in completion usage; absent usage details remain

E.2 Fixed-Input Multi-Round Review
Multi-round review lets agents revisit their initial answers using the same task observations
and scoring criteria. Self-review adds reconsideration, Crop review adds image crops, and
Tool-assisted review adds specialist visual and geometric measurements. The three conditions
permit up to two, two, and six additional responses, respectively, across Geometry, Spatial
Reasoning, and Affordance. Table 3a gives the results; the sampling and tools below define its
scope.
Shared cases. Astra and Qwen-27B share 46 cases: 19 Geometry, 18 Spatial Reasoning, and
nine Affordance. A 72-case pilot covers 24 benchmark/task strata selected by task category
and SHA-256 ordering with seed 20260923, independently of scores and answers. It excludes
the 60 exact cases from an earlier performance-selected study. One case per stratum forms a
24-case development set; withholding two more cases that share development scenes leaves
the 46 evaluation cases. The diagnostic includes recurring scenes and cases inspected during
development.
