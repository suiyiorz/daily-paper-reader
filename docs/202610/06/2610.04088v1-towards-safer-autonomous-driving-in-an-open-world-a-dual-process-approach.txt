# Towards Safer Autonomous Driving in an Open World: A Dual-Process Approach 

Simon Janssen, Michiel Braat 1, Chris van der Ploeg 1,3, Serge Thill 2, Jan-Pieter Paardekooper 1,2

Abstract — Before autonomous driving systems can be de-ployed on public roads, it is vital that these systems comply with safety standards, traffic rules, and social norms. Although neural networks trained on large amounts of driving data perform well in routine driving tasks, these models often struggle in novel situations that are not well-represented in the data. In this work, we propose a novel framework that combines a neural network for intuitive, learning-based plan-ning in routine driving tasks with model predictive control for reasoning-based planning in unfamiliar situations, inspired by Dual Process Theory. A meta-cognitive component is designed to switch between the two, using a knowledge graph to reason about contextual risk based on explicit perceptual information and relevant traffic rules and social norms. Contextual risk is represented through risk fields, guiding both the switching mechanism in the meta-cognitive component and compliance with safety standards, traffic rules, and social norms in the reasoning-based planner. The effectiveness of our framework is tested in CARLA for variations of a typical out-of-distribution situations involving (emergency) vehicles running a red light. We show that the novel architecture reduces the number of collisions in the scenarios by 89% and improves compliance with the special right-of-way rules, compared to the NN-only planner. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

To fully automate driving, Autonomous Driving Systems (ADSs) need to be able to navigate complex and dynamic environments while also ensuring safe and collision-free be-havior, obeying traffic rules, and taking into account driving requirements such as passenger comfort and route progres-sion. Advances in machine learning have enabled signifi-cant progress in addressing these challenges. While Neural Networks (NNs) have been used extensively in perception [1], they are also increasingly being explored for planning [2]. NN-based planners can potentially learn more complex and human-like driving behaviors that are difficult to capture directly through hand-crafted rules or objective functions [3]. Moreover, NNs enable end-to-end architectures, which unify perception, motion prediction, and planning [4], [5] , making them less prone to error propagation and misalignment issues arising from inconsistencies between different modules. Despite the large potential of NNs for planning, important challenges remain. One of these challenges is how to im-prove generalization performance and robustness in out-of-

To prevent serious accidents and fatalities in these situations, safe and predictable behavior is essential. Whereas NNs excel at finding statistical patterns between input and output, they often struggle to learn the deeper semantic meaning behind this input-output mapping. Hence, when faced with novel inputs, these networks may generalize learned patterns based on semantically irrelevant factors, leading to over-confident or incorrect predictions as a result. To facilitate adaptivity in the open world and improve safety, we propose a hybrid framework that combines the strengths of NNs and traditional planners. Specifically, an end-to-end network is used to generate complex and human-like trajectories in familiar situations, while an Model Pre-dictive Control (MPC)-based planner is used to explicitly take into account contextual information encoded in risk fields to comply with safety standards, legal constraints, and human values in out-of-distribution situations. The integra-tion of the two systems is guided by principles from Dual Process Theory [6], which similarly suggests that human intelligence arises from an intuitive system (System I) relying on prior experience, and a reasoning-based system (System II) engaging in more elaborate logical reasoning. To decide when to switch between System I and System II, a Meta-Cognitive Component (MCC) is designed that uses the same risk fields as the MPC-based planner to determine the safety and social desirability of the trajectory predicted by the end-to-end network. A. End-to-End Autonomous Driving 

End-to-end autonomous driving methods have recently emerged as a promising alternative to traditional modular pipelines, which separate perception, planning, and control. The two most prominent end-to-end approaches are imitation arXiv:2610.04088v1 [cs.AI] 2 Oct 2026 learning [4], [7] and reinforcement learning [5], [8]. Sev-eral methods directly optimize the planning task, omitting intermediate perception and motion prediction steps [9]. For example, CIL [4] uses a conditional imitation learning architecture to directly map an input image, input measure-ments, and a high-level control command to a corresponding output action. Other methods improve interpretability and robustness by learning auxiliary tasks such as object de-tection, semantic segmentation, depth estimation, and future occupancy prediction [7], [10]. 

B. Safety in Autonomous Driving 

A popular approach to improve the interpretability and safety of ADSs is to design explicit rules or cost functions that penalize collisions, deviations from the lane center, and violations of basic traffic rules, as well as other factors related to comfort and progress. Rules or cost functions can be integrated into the model architecture itself, such as in modular end-to-end architectures [11], [12]. They can also be applied externally as a separate safety system [13]. For example, SafetyNet employs a simple rule-based fallback layer that can modify the predicted trajectory of an end-to-end network when it is dynamically infeasible, violates traffic rules, or has a high likelihood of collision [3]. FusionAssurance determines the final safe trajectory using an MPC with a cost function that aims to follow the trajectory predicted by an end-to-end neural network, achieve the desired speed, improve comfort, and avoid obstacles modeled with risk functions [14]. Recently, Vision Language Models (VLMs) have been explored to act as a safety layer [15]. Unlike other meth-ods, VLMs can leverage their advanced world knowledge and reasoning capabilities to provide more context-aware interpretations of situations [16], which can subsequently be used to refine the trajectories generated by an end-to-end or modular architecture [17]. In this work, we also employ a reasoning-based system to act as a safety layer. However, we do not rely on VLMs, as these may be slow to respond and may suffer from hallucinations [16]. Instead, we follow the work in [18] and use an MPC-based planner and a knowledge graph to generate context-aware trajectories. Although this may result in less human-like behavior, this shortcoming is acceptable when used as a safety system, where the main objective is to ensure safe and socially desirable behavior. 

C. Trajectory Evaluation 

A vital aspect of the dual system architecture is the decision when to switch between the NN-based planner and MPC-based planner. This ties into the concept of meta-cognition, as it requires knowing when the NN-based planner cannot make a reliable decision [19]. Previous work has explored approaches that switch control when a trajectory scoring function exceeds a pre-defined threshold value [13]. A key advantage of using a trajectory scoring function is that individual predictions can be evaluated on actual measures of risk or reward, rather than approximate measures such as novelty or uncertainty [20]. Approximate measures often implicitly assume that end-to-end networks always perform reliably or poorly in certain situations, which may not hold in practice. In this work, we design an MCC that uses risk fields in the trajectory scoring function to decide when to switch to the MPC-based planner. Unlike other trajectory scoring functions [11], [12], [13], contextual information derived from a knowledge graph is used to shape these risk fields. This allows us to detect not only potential collisions, but also applications of more complex traffic rules and social norms. The proposed trajectory scoring function intentionally excludes comfort and route progression measures, as these factors are not directly relevant to determining the safety of a trajectory and in this work not important enough to switch system. III. M ETHOD 

The proposed dual-process framework consists of three main components, as illustrated in Fig. 1. A NN-based planner is used to predict an initial solution to the plan-ning problem based on available input data from camera, LiDAR, and GPS sensors. This solution is then passed to a MCC, which evaluates its safety and desirability and decides whether to initiate a switch to a MPC-based planner. When engaged, the MPC-based planner can explicitly take into account safety, traffic rules, and social norms encompassed in the risk fields to produce a safer and more socially desirable trajectory at the expense of the ability to generate human-like trajectories. 

A. System I (Neural Network) 

In this work, we adopt TransFuser++ as the end-to-end planner, using the codebase and pre-trained model weights made publicly available by the authors [7]. The model uses a multi-modal fusion transformer to integrate information from a front-facing high-resolution RGB camera (3 x 384 x 1024) and 360 ◦ field of view LiDAR (voxelized into a 256 x 256 grid). Subsequently, it predicts a target speed and ten future path positions with a fixed distance of one meter between them, conditioned on a goal location (GPS coordinates) provided by a global planner and the current speed. A Proportional Integral Derivative (PID) controller converts this information to low-level driving commands (steering, throttle, brake). The model learns four auxiliary tasks to improve robustness: semantic segmentation, Bird’s Eye View (BEV) semantic segmentation, depth estimation, and bounding-box object detection. We refer the reader to [7] and [21] for more details on the architecture and training procedure.

2) Knowledge Graph: To model contextual understand-ing, relevant information about the current driving situation is organized and linked using a knowledge graph. First, percep-tual information is added to the knowledge graph, including object states o(j)0 , object classifications c(j), infrastructure information, and relations between them. Next, reasoning rules are used to add implicit information about traffic rules and social norms. Specifically, rules are used to infer the acceptability of crossing lane markings, the risk levels of objects, the permissibility to drive in a particular lane, and the precedence at intersections. The knowledge graph and reasoning rules are imple-mented using TypeDB 3.0. An ontology defines the semantic relationship between concepts through entities, relations, and attributes. Entities represent independent concepts, like detected objects, lanes, or traffic signs. Relations describe interactions between entities by assigning them roles. For instance, the ‘in front’ relation links a subject (ego entity) and an object (actor entity). Attributes assign values to entities or relations, representing properties such as speed or position. 4) Evaluating Predicted Path: To evaluate risk along the path predicted by TransFuser++, it is converted into a time-based trajectory that encodes the position of the ego vehicle at each time step k for each k ∈ [0 ...N ], where N is the prediction horizon. Cubic spline interpolation is used to cre-ate a smooth curve between the predicted path points. Next, the ego vehicle motion is simulated using a longitudinal PID controller (to predict throttle and brake inputs) and a learned polynomial model (to estimate future velocities). Since the ten predicted future path positions only cover 12.5 meters in front of the ego vehicle, the maximum prediction horizon 

N dynamically reduces when the ego vehicle is moving fast, such that the final position remains within the predicted 12.5-meter path. Trajectories of surrounding objects are predicted using the Constant Velocity model for pedestrians and the Constant Steering Angle and Velocity model for vehicles [24]. C. System II (Model Predictive Control) 

The MPC-based planner follows the implementation of [22] and [18], optimising a path of minimal risk towards the goal point. The planner uses a non-linear kinematic bicycle model, suitable for urban driving, and input bounds based on the properties of the ego vehicle. The optimization objective of the MPC combines this model, the current state, the risk fields (1), (2), (3), (4), and (5), and a reference state rN ,similar to Eq. 12 in [18]. The planar position of rN is provided by the global planner (i.e., the same global planner as is used by TransFuser++). Having the risk fields as basis for the optimization objective ensures that the planner takes into account the traffic rules and social norms that form the basis for the risk fields. The planner is implemented using the CasADi [25] op-timization framework together with the IPOPT non-linear solver [26]. The planner optimizes trajectories over the max-imum prediction horizon N . For low-level vehicle control, two PID controllers are used, identical to the controllers used in [7]. IV. R ESULTS

B. Results 

Collisions that occur in the simulations are shown in Table XI. The TransFuser++ model without the MCC collides with the adversary vehicle in 48 out of 160 simulations, while the system with the MCC results in only 5 collisions. This is similar to the state-of-the-art TFv6 model which had 8 collisions. Fig. 6 shows the number of vehicle-to-vehicle collisions per scenario parameter. As expected, the model with the MCC produces fewer collisions with the adversary vehicle. With the MCC active, the risk of entering the intersection when an ambulance is approaching or when the adversary vehicle is crossing exceeds the threshold, which makes the MPC take over control and stop the vehicle. An example of this is shown in Fig. 7. The MCC also enables System II to intervene in near-collision situations, as the risk around nearby road users becomes sufficiently high. An example of this behavior is shown in Fig. 8. These examples illustrate the benefit of integrating the MCC and reasoning-based System II: the system explicitly recognizes high-risk situations or potential violations of traffic rules and, even in out-of-distribution cases, prevents unnecessary collisions. Both the TransFuser++ model with and without the MCC perform worst at intersection D. This is likely because, in this configuration, the adversary vehicle approaches from the front rather than from the left or right, making it more difficult for both systems to anticipate and react to the hazard. Furthermore, all road-layout collisions for the model with the MCC occur in Scenario 4. In this work, we propose a novel dual-process approach that combines an NN-based planner and a context-aware MPC-based planner. We designed an MCC that uses risk fields shaped by perceptual information, traffic rules, social norms, and predicts trajectories of surrounding objects to evaluate the safety and social desirability of a trajectory generated by an end-to-end neural network.
