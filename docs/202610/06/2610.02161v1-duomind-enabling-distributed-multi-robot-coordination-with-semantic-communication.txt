ABSTRACT
Vision–language models (VLMs) and vision–language–action models (VLAs)
have recently driven rapid progress in general-purpose robots, yet most progress
has focused on single-robot settings. Extending these capabilities to multi-robot
systems remains challenging because robots must coordinate long-horizon behav-
iors while maintaining reliable, fine-grained execution. We introduce DuoMind, a
distributed hierarchical framework for multi-robot coordination through semantic
communication. Each robot uses a VLA-based action model for low-level execu-
tion and a VLM-based orchestrator for high-level reasoning and inter-agent coor-
dination. At each planning step, the orchestrator at each robot reasons over the task
instruction, local observations, and messages received from other robots. It then
generates low-level instructions for the action model and semantic messages for
peer robots. This architecture exploits the complementary strengths of pretrained
models by combining the semantic reasoning capabilities of VLMs with the pre-
cise action-generation capabilities of VLAs. To address the scarcity of bench-
marks for multi-robot coordination, we further develop RoboPoly, a benchmark
comprising long-horizon manipulation tasks that require coordinated, closed-loop
execution under distributed control. Experiments on RoboPoly and RoboTwin
demonstrate that DuoMind improves multi-robot task performance, while abla-
tion studies confirm the contributions of hierarchical orchestration and semantic
communication. More details are available on our project page.

INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) and vision-language-action models (VLAs)
have substantially expanded the capabilities of general-purpose robots. VLMs (Beyer et al., 2024;
Yang et al., 2025) provide strong visual understanding and high-level reasoning, while VLAs trans-
late visual observations and natural-language instructions into robot actions (Zitkovich et al., 2023;
Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024; Intelligence et al., 2025b; Team et al., 2025). Building on
these complementary capabilities, recent design of robot agents (Song et al., 2025a; Intelligence
et al., 2025b; Team et al., 2025; Intelligence et al., 2025a) combines a high-level embodied reason-
ing model with a low-level VLA policy. In this design, the reasoning model aims to understand
the environment and then decompose complex tasks into more specific instructions, while the VLA
executes these instructions through fine-grained robot actions. Such a separation of functionalities
allows the high-level model to focus on semantic understanding and long-horizon planning, while
the VLA specializes in executing detailed instructions with precise low-level control.
Notably, most of the above progress has focused on a single robot acting in isolation, but many
practical tasks are infeasible for a single agent and inherently require multi-robot collaboration, and
others can benefit from multiple robots working together to improve efficiency. Effective multi-
robot coordination requires each robot to construct appropriate subgoals and continuously adapt
its behavior as the shared workspace evolves. For many practical settings, distributed execution
(Amirkhani & Barshooi, 2022; Lo et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024a) is particularly desirable, where
each robot makes decisions primarily from its own observations rather than relying on a centralized
controller, making the system more scalable as the number of robots increases (Nayak et al., 2023).
Yet combining distributed multi-agent decision making with complex robotic manipulation creates
a particularly challenging coordination problem, requiring robots to reason about both team-level
cooperation and precise low-level task execution.
Conventional multi-agent learning and planning methods are often studied in relatively simple envi-
ronments (Feng et al., 2026; Li et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025; Wong et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2025a),
with limited physical interaction and shorter task horizons. More recently, language-model-based
robotic systems have leveraged the high-level reasoning capabilities of LLMs to decompose com-
plex objectives and schedule robot behaviors (Chen et al., 2024b; He et al., 2026; Song et al., 2025b;
Liu et al., 2025; Mandi et al., 2024). However, most of these systems focus primarily on high-level
planning, while low-level control is executed through simply predefined action primitives or rule-
based skills. An important gap remains between high-level multi-agent reasoning and end-to-end
robot control. In addition, existing benchmarks provide limited support for systematic evaluation
of multi-robot coordination, leaving the field without a comprehensive testbed for measuring such
capabilities.
To address the above challenges, we introduce DuoMind, a distributed hierarchical framework for
multi-agent robotic coordination, together with RoboPoly, a benchmark designed specifically for
long-horizon multi-robot coordination. Each robot in DuoMind operates with a VLM as its orches-
trator for high-level reasoning and a VLA as its action model for low-level control. At each planning
step, each orchestrator reasons over the high-level instruction, its local observation, and the shared
message from other robots. It then generates both a low-level instruction for the action model and a
structured message for other robots. These two outputs serve complementary roles. The low-level
instruction guides the action model to produce fine-grained robot actions, while the message com-
municates the robot’s intention, subgoal, and task belief, allowing each agent to remain aware of
its peers despite partial observability (Liang et al., 2025b) in the distributed setting. In this way,
DuoMind provides an agentic harness around the action model: the orchestrator dynamically rea-
sons and invokes the action model to realize long-horizon coordinated behavior at a relatively low
frequency, while the action model focuses on high-frequency execution. Importantly, this decoupled
design allows DuoMind to capitalize on the strong pretrained capabilities of existing VLM and VLA
backbones. Rather than heavily fine-tuning models specifically for multi-robot coordination, high-
level coordination and low-level manipulation can be largely handled by off-the-shelf VLM models,
requiring only limited adaptation to the multi-robot setting. To systematically evaluate the coordi-
nation capabilities, we introduce RoboPoly, which provides challenging long-horizon manipulation
tasks in which multiple robots must coordinate and complete the task through closed-loop physical
execution rather than high-level planning alone. We further evaluate DuoMind across diverse co-
2


operative scenarios and systematically study the contributions of hierarchical design and inter-agent
communication.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
• We introduce DuoMind, a distributed hierarchical framework for end-to-end multi-agent
robotic coordination. DuoMind utilizes a VLM as the orchestrator at each robot for high-
level reasoning and a VLA as the action model for low-level control, enabling inter-agent
semantic communication with natural language. This design harnesses the strong reasoning
and control capabilities of existing foundation models to enable effective coordination in
complex multi-robot tasks.
• We develop RoboPoly, a benchmark for long-horizon multi-robot coordination under dis-
tributed observations and control. The benchmark emphasizes closed-loop physical execu-
tion and includes tasks that are challenging for a single agent, requiring multiple agents to
coordinate and schedule their behaviors throughout task execution.
• We conduct extensive experiments and ablation studies to evaluate DuoMind across chal-
lenging cooperative manipulation tasks on both RoboPoly and RoboTwin. Our results
demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed hierarchical design in multi-robot coordi-
nation and further quantify the contributions of integrating orchestrator and inter-agent
communication.

DUOMIND: DISTRIBUTED HIERARCHICAL MULTI-AGENT FRAMEWORK
We introduce DuoMind, a distributed hierarchical framework for multi-agent robotic coordination.
We first present the VLM-based orchestrator for high-level planning (2.1). We then describe how
we bridges high-level planning and low-level control via embodied reasoning and agentic harness
(2.2). Finally, we highlight the structured inter-agent communication (2.3).
Figure 2: In DuoMind, each robot operates with a VLM as its orchestrator for high-level reasoning
and a VLA as its action model for low-level control. At each high-level planning step, the orches-

structured message for other robots. The action model subsequently follows the low-level instruc-
tion and produces fine-grained actions.
2.1
MULTI-AGENT COORDINATION VIA EMBODIED REASONING
Existing studies on hierarchical robotic architectures have shown that action models are effective
at low-level control and instruction following when provided with specific commands (Team et al.,
2025). To extend this capability toward more general and long-horizon tasks, a promising approach
is to introduce a reasoning model as an orchestrator, leveraging its strong visual understanding,
semantic grounding, and planning capabilities to generate appropriate instructions for the action
model. In distributed multi-agent settings, where task coordination are substantially more complex,
we extend this hierarchical paradigm to distributed multi-robot coordination in DuoMind.
3


Specifically, DuoMind decomposes multi-agent coordination into two complementary levels: high-
level multi-agent reasoning and low-level execution. Each robot employs a VLM as an orchestrator
that reasons over the task-level instruction, its local visual observation, and message shared by other
agents. The orchestrator determines the robot’s current subtask and translates it into a single-agent,
short-horizon instruction for low-level control. The VLA, serving as the action model, then follows
the low-level instruction using local observations to generate fine-grained robot actions. This divi-
sion of responsibility allows each module to focus on the capability for which it is best suited: the
orchestrator handles high-level understanding and inter-agent coordination in multi-agent setting,
while the action model performs reliable low-level control.
Importantly, this decomposition avoids requiring the action model to directly interpret complex
multi-agent state that includes the joint behavior of multiple robots and environment, which can
be extremely challenging since action models are primarily trained with single-agent demonstra-
tions. It therefore decouples multi-agent reasoning from low-level control, reducing the need for the
action model itself to possess strong reasoning capabilities for multi-agent coordination. Moreover,
DuoMind does not require the orchestrator, the action model, or the interface between them to be ex-
tensively redesigned for multi-agent settings. These foundation models developed for single-agent
systems can be reused largely off the shelf, with only lightweight adaptation when necessary. This
greatly extends the possibility of deploying DuoMind to diverse multi-robot tasks and offers strong
compatibility to future upgrades on components.
The design of DuoMind naturally supports coordination among multiple robots. However, the com-
plexity of task design, data collection, and evaluation increases substantially as the number of agents
grows. Considering the high computational cost when the number of robots scales up, we focus on
two-agent settings in this work and leave coordination at larger team scales for future study.
2.2
BRIDGING HIGH-LEVEL REASONING AND LOW-LEVEL CONTROL VIA NATURAL
LANGUAGE BASED SEMANTIC COMMUNICATION
Hierarchical robotic architectures bridge high-level reasoning and low-level control through differ-
ent intermediate representations, including natural-language subgoals or motion descriptions (Shi
et al., 2025; Belkhale et al., 2024), spatial representations such as trajectories or visual subgoals (Li
et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025), and learned latent representations in tightly coupled dual-system
models (Chen et al., 2025a; Bjorck et al., 2025). Among these approaches, natural language offers
interpretability and broad compatibility with language-conditioned models, whereas other represen-
tations can convey compact information but typically require stronger coupling between the two
modules, making them harder to replace or adapt across architectures and embodiments.
Our framework adopts natural language as a unified interface between the high-level orchestrator and
the low-level action model. The orchestrator harnesses the action model by generating low-level
instructions in natural language, thereby decoupling high-level reasoning from low-level control.
This design allows existing VLMs and VLAs to be incorporated as off-the-shelf components with
minimal adaptation, without requiring them to be jointly trained through a specialized interface. It
also enables either component to be independently replaced as stronger reasoning or action models
become available.
Natural language further provides a common interface for inter-agent semantic communication.
Each orchestrator can interpret messages from other agents even when they use different reason-
ing models, facilitating coordination across heterogeneous agents. The same interface also supports
human participation by allowing users to inspect or revise instructions using ordinary language.
More broadly, this shared linguistic interface provides a scalable foundation for future multi-robot
systems, where heterogeneous robots and multiple human users can coordinate through a common
communication protocol.
2.3
INTER-AGENT INFORMATION SHARING
Distributed multi-agent systems face the challenges of partial observability and non-stationarity:
each agent has only partial knowledge of the global task state, while the environment continues
to evolve as other agents act. Insufficient awareness of such information can lead to conflicting
actions or ineffective collaboration. To address these challenges, DuoMind enables agents to share
4


and integrate structured natural-language messages during high-level reasoning. These messages
convey key information about other agents’ decisions and task-relevant changes in the environment,
providing additional context for more precise high-level reasoning. To support effective decision-
making, we structure each shared message using four fields:
Intention. It is identical to the low-level instruction sent to the action model and describes the next
subtask that the ego agent intends to execute. Sharing this intention makes other agents aware of the
ego agent’s next action, helping them avoid behaviors that may interfere with its execution.
Subgoals. This field specifies the expected task state after the ego agent completes its current sub-
task. It explicitly communicates the future state that the ego agent aims to achieve, allowing other
agents to act proactively to facilitate task completion or avoid undesirable outcomes.
Belief of task. This part describes task-relevant information observed or inferred by the ego agent,
such as the location of a key object or whether a particular subtask has been completed. For example,
in a kitchen task, a robot operating near the cabinets may inform another robot preparing to cook that
“the pot is in the third cabinet.” Sharing such information reduces the impact of partial observability
by propagating locally available knowledge across agents.
Uncertainty of belief. This optional field communicates the uncertainty associated with the ego
agent’s task belief. It is particularly useful in complex tasks, where information inferred through
reasoning may not always be reliable. This uncertainty provides an additional confidence signal
that allows the orchestrator to appropriately weigh different beliefs during high-level reasoning,
especially when agents hold inconsistent beliefs or when the available information is ambiguous.
Through this structured information sharing, DuoMind provides each agent with compact contextual
information about the intentions, subgoals, beliefs, and associated uncertainty of other agents. This
additional context enables the orchestrator to generate more precise low-level instructions, thereby
improving the safety and efficiency of multi-robot coordination.

ROBOPOLY
RoboPoly is designed to facilitate research on multi-robot manipulation by providing challenging
long-horizon tasks with explicit cooperation requirements. Unlike RoboTwin Chen et al. (2025c),
which primarily targets dual-arm manipulation with two arms on the same embodiment and does
not necessarily require explicit cooperation, RoboPoly considers two independent robots and tasks
specifically designed around multi-robot coordination. These tasks are either infeasible or inefficient
for a single robot to complete independently, requiring each robot to reason and determine when
cooperation is needed, and act at the appropriate time.
RoboPoly highlights distributed execution, where each robot acts based on its own observations and
policy while cooperating with another robot. The benchmark is built upon the ManiSkill3 framework
and contains seven multi-robot manipulation tasks. In this section, we describe the task suite, basic
settings, and dataset generation.
3.1
TASKS FOR MULTI-ROBOT COORDINATION
All tasks share a common workspace configuration with two robot arms positioned on opposite sides
of a table. The workspace and object placement are designed such that a single robot cannot always
complete the task independently or efficiently, making cooperation necessary. RoboPoly contains
the following seven tasks: Food Serve, Prepare Snack, Clean Table, Cook Pot, Put Object Cabinet,
Hang Bag, Exchange Bread. We further detail the task description in A.
3.2
OBSERVATIONS, ACTIONS, AND ROBOTS
Each environment contains three RGB cameras: one global camera with a resolution of 512 × 384
and two wrist cameras with a resolution of 128 × 128. The global camera is positioned beside the
table to provide an overview of the workspace, while each robot is equipped with a wrist camera that
provides an egocentric view of its gripper. We adopt a distributed observation setting in which each
robot receives observations from the global camera and its own wrist camera, but has no access to
5


the wrist camera of the other robot. This design follows the distributed system setting and requires
the robots to coordinate using only their locally available observations.
Both robots use 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda manipulators equipped with grippers. Under the dis-
tributed control setting, each policy controls only one robot rather than directly generating the joint
actions of both robots. To improve task diversity, key objects are randomized within predefined
task-specific regions at the beginning of each episode.
3.3
DATASETS
We construct the datasets using the motion-planning-based trajectory generation pipeline provided
by ManiSkill3. For each task, we first decompose the full manipulation procedure into a sequence
of key states, such as grasping, releasing, or moving toward a target location. Given these key states,
the generation script invokes the motion planner to construct smooth robot trajectories between
consecutive states. The spatial and temporal constraints for each trajectories are carefully designed
with the planner to generate the desired multi-robot coordination demonstrations.
In addition to robot trajectories, we associate each manipulation stage with a low-level instruction
for each robot describing the current behavior. We also provide a high-level instruction that specifies
the overall task objective. During data generation, the pipeline first plans a successful trajectory and
records the corresponding robot actions and states. It then replays the trajectory in simulator to
render the RGB camera observations. The resulting dataset therefore contains visual observations,
states, robot actions, high-level, and low-level language instructions for both robots. We further
open-source the dataset to foster research on multi-robot coordination.

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate DuoMind on 7 tasks from our RoboPoly and 8 dual-arm tasks from RoboTwin 2.0
(Chen et al., 2025c), both under distributed control. Since RoboTwin originally adopts centralized
control over the dual-arm robot, we adapt its setting by treating the two arms as independent robots
and control them separately. Task overview are shown in fig. 5 and fig. 6. All the finetuning and
evaluation tasks are conducted on 4 NVIDIA A100 GPU, each taking 10–24 hours.
In the RoboPoly experiments, each Franka Emika Panda robot has 7 DoF and is equipped with a
wrist camera, while both robots share a global camera positioned beside the workspace. We adopt
the same distributed setting in which each robot has its own observations and policy. For each
task, we collect 50 dual-robot demonstrations using planning-based trajectory generation and then
decompose them into a unified single-robot dataset containing 100 trajectories for finetuning.
On RoboTwin, we select the tasks that require the coordination of two arms for multi-robot exper-
iment. We treat the dual-arm Aloha-AgileX as two individual robots, each operating with its own
observations and independently controlled by a separate policy. Each arm has 6 DoF with a wrist
camera and a shared head camera. At finetuning stage, we use 50 official dual-arm demonstrations
on each task. Each demonstration is decomposed into separate left- and right-arm trajectories, where
each trajectory is paired with observations from the corresponding wrist camera and the shared head
camera. We then merge the trajectories from both arms into a unified single-arm dataset, resulting
in 100 demonstrations per task.

the baselines over 400 rollouts per task and report the corresponding success rates in table 2 and

4.2
MAIN RESULTS
We evaluate DuoMind on seven tasks in RoboPoly and eight tasks in RoboTwin against strong base-
lines, with success rates reported in table 1 and table 2, respectively. Detailed task rollout examples
are provided in section E. Overall, DuoMind matches or outperforms the baseline methods across
most tasks. We attribute these gains to the high-level reasoning and inter-agent communication
enabled by the hierarchical design.
For long-horizon multi-robot tasks, a global high-level instruction provides only coarse guidance
throughout the entire task execution and offers limited information about what each robot should do
at the current stage or how it should adapt to the behavior of its peers. With the hierarchical design,
in contrast, the orchestrator reasons over the current task state together with messages from other
agents, decomposes the task into appropriate subtasks, and provides specific low-level instructions
to the action model. This allows each robot to adapt its behavior as the task evolves while avoiding
conflicts with other agents.

can focus on executing the assigned subtask rather than implicitly reasoning about the overall task
progression and multi-agent coordination. As shown in fig. 3, the robots coordinate effectively and

4.4
ACTION MODEL COMPATIBILITY
DuoMind decouples high-level orchestration from low-level control, allowing different action mod-
els to be integrated into the framework. To study the influence of the action model, we evaluate
DuoMind with π0 in place of π0.5. As shown in fig. 4b, since π0 is a weaker action model than π0.5,
DuoMind with π0 achieves lower success rates than DuoMind with π0.5, as expected. Nevertheless,
it substantially outperforms the π0-only baseline, demonstrating that the hierarchical orchestration
of DuoMind consistently improves multi-robot coordination across different action models. At the
same time, the stronger action model provides more reliable low-level execution, which further im-
proves the overall task success rate. We detail this analysis in C.

RELATED WORK
5.1
LEARNING-BASED MULTI-AGENT COORDINATION
Classical multi-agent learning studies decentralized decision-making under partial observability
and non-stationarity through centralized training, value decomposition, and learned communica-
tion (Lowe et al., 2017; Rashid et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2022; Foerster et al., 2016). These methods
are effective in cooperative games and simulation, but often require task-specific joint training and
encode coordination in latent representations that are hard to interpret or integrate with pretrained
robot foundation models. Recent work extends coordination to physical robots through decentral-
ized imitation learning, shared representations, and VLA policies (Dong et al., 2025; He et al., 2025;
Qin et al., 2025; Doshi et al., 2026). In contrast, DuoMind uses decentralized reasoning with explicit
natural-language communication for interpretable exchange of task-relevant information.
5.2
ROBOT LEARNING AND MULTI-ROBOT PLANNING
Generalist robot policies leverage large-scale robot data and pretrained vision/language representa-
tions to map observations or language instructions to actions (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024;
Black et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2026; Team et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026). Hierarchical approaches
further separate high-level semantic reasoning from low-level continuous control (Shi et al., 2025;
Intelligence et al., 2025b; Song et al., 2025a; Team et al., 2025), but mainly focus on single-robot
execution. LLM- and VLM-based multi-robot systems explore task decomposition, allocation, com-
munication, feedback, and replanning (Kannan et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024b; Mandi et al., 2024;
Liu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2024; He et al., 2026), while typically relying
on predefined skills, symbolic actions, waypoints, or motion planners. DuoMind instead combines
distributed high-level reasoning and communication with VLA-based physical execution.
5.3
ROBOT LEARNING AND MULTI-AGENT BENCHMARKS
Established robot-learning benchmarks provide diverse manipulation tasks, demonstrations, lan-
guage instructions, and long-horizon evaluation, but largely focus on single robots or centrally con-
trolled embodiments (Yu et al., 2020; James et al., 2020; Gu et al., 2023; Mees et al., 2022; Liu
et al., 2023). Recent multi-agent benchmarks study collaborative planning, heterogeneous capabili-
ties, and manipulation (Mandi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025b; Chang et al., 2025;
Qin et al., 2025). However, they often emphasize either abstract high-level planning or low-level ma-
nipulation. RoboPoly instead evaluates long-horizon coordination and closed-loop execution under
distributed observation and control.

CONCLUSION
We presented DuoMind, a distributed hierarchical framework for multi-robot coordination that com-
bines high-level reasoning, low-level control, and semantic inter-agent communication. We further
introduced RoboPoly, a benchmark for evaluating long-horizon multi-robot manipulation under dis-
tributed observations and control. Experiments on RoboPoly and RoboTwin demonstrate that hi-
erarchical orchestration improves multi-robot task execution, while ablation studies highlight the
importance of explicit information sharing for coordination under partial observability. The decou-

TASK SETTINGS OF ROBOPOLY
RoboPoly contains the following seven tasks:
Food Serve. Two pieces of bread and a serving tray are placed across the workspace such that one
robot cannot reach both the bread and the tray. The robots must transfer the bread between each
other when necessary and place both pieces onto the tray.
Prepare Snack. Two plates are placed near the center of the table, while four pieces of bread are
distributed across the two sides of the workspace. The robots must place two pieces on each plate.
Both robots can operate simultaneously and must coordinate their motions to efficiently complete
the task while avoiding collisions.
Clean Table. Two cubes and a container are placed on the table such that neither robot can inde-
pendently reach all cubes and container. Completing the task requires a cube to be passed between
the robots when the container lies outside the workspace of a robot.
Cook Pot. A carrot, a pot with a lid, and an oven are placed around the center of the workspace.
The robots must first remove the lid, place the carrot into the pot, and replace the lid. They should
then jointly grasp the two handles of the pot and transport it to the oven.
13


Put Object Cabinet. A cube and a cabinet are placed in the workspace. The robots must open
the bottom drawer of the cabinet, place the cube inside, and close the drawer. The task requires
coordination between two robot arms for higher efficiency.
Hang Bag. A bag and a hook are placed on opposite sides of the workspace such that neither robot
can reach both. One robot must first pass the bag to the other robot, which then hangs the bag onto
the hook.
Exchange Bread. A piece of bread and a target box are placed beside each robot arm. Each robot
must place its own bread into the box on the opposite side of the table. Since the target box lies
outside its reachable workspace, the robot must first hand the bread over to the other robot, which
then places it into the corresponding box.

EFFECT OF INTER-AGENT COMMUNICATION
Inter-agent communication shares complementary information for decision making and helps miti-
gate the partial observability inherent in distributed multi-robot settings. To further study the effect
of the shared messages, we conduct an ablation using DuoMind with π0.5 as the action model, fol-
lowing the evaluation setting in section 4.2 except that messages from peer agents are disabled.
Under this setting, each orchestrator receives no information from the other agent during reasoning
and generates low-level instructions based only on its local observation and the task instruction.
The results are reported in fig. 7. Without inter-agent communication, we observe more frequent
conflicts and asynchronous behaviors between the two robots. For example, in Cook Pot, the two
14


Figure 7: The result of ablation study that disable the inter-agent communication in DuoMind.
robots are required to lift the pot handles in a coordinated manner. Without communication, one
robot often starts lifting while the other has only begun grasping the handle, causing the coordinated
manipulation to fail. In Prepare Snack, the two robots may independently select the same plate as
their target and attempt to place bread simultaneously, which can lead to collisions between the robot
arms above the plate.
These failure cases illustrate a limitation of relying solely on local observations: an individual agent
may not have sufficient information to infer the peer agent’s current intention or upcoming behav-
ior. Explicit inter-agent communication provides a direct and efficient mechanism for sharing such
information, thereby reducing conflicts and improving synchronized and safe multi-robot coordina-
tion.

VLM SYSTEM PROMPT
We guide the orchestrator with a general system prompt shared across agents and tasks, which spec-
ifies its role and the type of low-level instruction to generate for the action model. The orchestrator
serves two roles. First, it performs high-level planning by reasoning over the ego agent’s reachable
workspace, the peer agent’s intention, and the current task state. It selects a complementary subgoal
that avoids conflicts with other agents and contributes effectively to the overall task. Second, it gen-
erates a structured message for inter-agent communication, providing concise information about the
ego agent’s intention, belief, and uncertainty to support the peer agent’s reasoning and coordination.
