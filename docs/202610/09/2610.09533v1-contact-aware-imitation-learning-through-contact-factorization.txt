# Contact-Aware Imitation Learning Through Contact Factorization 

Jiho Hong 1, Daeun Song 2∗, Sanghyun Kim 1∗, Mingyo Seo 3∗

Abstract — Generalizable contact-rich manipulation requires robots to preserve intended task behavior while adapting its physical realization to changing contact conditions. However, interaction forces can vary substantially with small changes in surface geometry, orientation, and friction, making policies trained directly on raw force measurements difficult to trans-fer beyond demonstrated conditions. We introduce FACE, a contact-factorized imitation learning framework that separates intended task behavior from environment-dependent contact factors. Our representation expresses interaction forces in nor-malized, contact-relative coordinates, while a learned contact-normal estimator and an online friction estimator infer the local contact normal and effective friction scale. Together, these estimators enable force observations to be encoded and policy outputs to be decoded into physical motion and force commands during execution. In this way, FACE adapts execution to current contact conditions while preserving the intended task behavior, without updating the policy parameters. We evaluate FACE on real-robot contact-rich manipulation under unseen variations in surface properties and geometry, demonstrating robust generalization across contact conditions through con-trolled comparisons with variants that adapt prior approaches to our setting. Videos and additional materials can be found on the project page: https://rcilab.khu.ac.kr/face .

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in robot learning have enabled robots to acquire complex manipulation behaviors from scalable human demonstrations [1–4]. This progress has been strong for tasks in which visual observations provide much of the information needed to choose an action. However, contact-rich manipulation also depends on physical properties of the environment that may not be known before interaction. The same intended behavior can require different forces across surfaces or objects, and those forces may need to be applied along different directions as the contact geometry changes. Consequently, directly imitating raw force measurements can tie a learned policy to the conditions seen during demonstra-tion rather than the interaction required by the task. Force/torque and tactile feedback have enabled force-aware imitation learning, tactile-guided control, adaptive compliance, and multimodal policy learning for contact-rich manipulation [5–11]. These methods substantially improve contact awareness and robustness, but they generally repre-sent forces in physical quantities whose scale and orientation depend on the environment. During sliding, the required tan-gential force varies with friction and normal loading, while force directions vary with surface orientation. A. Force-Aware Demonstration and Imitation Learning 

Recent work has increasingly incorporated physical inter-action signals into imitation learning. One common approach acquires force-aware demonstrations through teleoperation and enables policies to attend explicitly to interaction feed-back [8]. While effective, such systems require an instru-mented human-robot interface and closed-loop robot execu-tion during demonstration, and the recorded force signals remain tied to the physical conditions under which they are collected. Complementary to teleoperation-based collection, robot-free and human-centered interfaces enable demonstration collection through handheld tools and direct human interac-tion [5, 12–16]. More recent systems extend this paradigm to capture interaction forces during demonstration and learn force-aware manipulation policies [6, 7]. These approaches facilitate natural acquisition of coupled motion and force information and improve contact regulation. Their learned force and compliance targets can nevertheless remain de-pendent on the contact conditions represented in the demon-strations. Beyond data collection, recent visuomotor policies incor-porate force or tactile sensing through reactive architectures, multimodal fusion, and slow-fast policy structures [17–19]. Concurrent work also analyzes characteristic failure modes of force-aware vision-language-action policies in contact-rich tasks [20]. Together, these works establish the importance of physical interaction feedback. Our focus is on how that interaction is represented across changing contact conditions. 

B. Contact-Aware Action Representation 

Beyond force-aware observation and demonstration, an-other key question is how contact-rich actions should be rep-resented and executed. Classical force-control methods struc-ture contact-rich actions by separating force- and motion-controlled directions in task-related coordinates [21, 22], while later learning-based approaches infer changing con-straint frames from demonstrations [23]. Learning-based work has also explored action representations that expose physically meaningful control variables, such as end-effector motion together with variable impedance, to improve learn-ing in contact-rich tasks [24]. More recent policies learn task-dependent compliance or force regulation [25, 26], and combine learned visuomotor policies with interaction-frame estimation or hybrid force-position execution [27, 28]. These approaches improve contact stability and force regulation through learned compliance and contact-aware execution. We focus on normalizing tangential loading by friction-scaled normal loading to represent interaction across changing con-tact conditions. Other methods use physical feedback at inference time to calibrate predicted states or steer generated actions toward safer or more feasible interactions [29–31]. Concurrent work further explores fast force reflexes, asynchronous vision-force policy composition, and object-centric contact-wrench guidance [32–34]. Our approach uses the estimated contact normal and friction scale to encode force observations in contact-factorized coordinates and decode the outputs of a fixed policy into physical motion and force commands. III. M ETHOD 

We introduce FACE (Force Adaptation through Contact-Factor Estimation), an imitation-learning framework for contact-rich manipulation using a contact-factorized rep-resentation. The key idea is to represent environment-dependent physical interaction through a compact contact factor, separating it from the task behavior demonstrated by the user, as illustrated in Fig. 1. A visuomotor policy there-fore learns receding-horizon actions in contact-factorized coordinates, while the contact factor is estimated by aseparate module that requires no task-specific supervision. At deployment, online contact-factor estimates transform the outputs of a fixed policy into pose and force references consistent with the current contact condition. 

A. Problem Formulation 

We formulate contact-rich manipulation of objects and surfaces with unknown physical properties and local contact geometry as a partially observed Markov decision process

The contact factor is generally unknown before interaction and may vary across objects, surfaces, and contact locations. B. Contact Factorization for Interaction Representation 

Following UMI [14], we express pose observations and motion actions relative to the current end-effector pose. This removes dependence on the absolute robot configuration and world frame, allowing the policy to represent motion locally with respect to the task. All force quantities and the contact normal nt are expressed in the current end-effector frame. Resolving the measured force with respect to nt then expresses interaction relative to the local surface rather than fixed end-effector axes. We use a unit contact normal oriented so that compressive normal loading is positive, and obtain normal and tangential forces by projecting the measured force onto this normal and its orthogonal plane. D. Online Contact Estimation & Execution Adaptation 

At deployment, we estimate zt = (nt, μ t) online to convert outputs of a fixed policy into motion and force references for the hybrid controller under unseen contact conditions. We first estimate the contact state and local geometry. The contact activation αt ∈ [0 , 1] is computed by applying a smoothstep function to the measured force magnitude between prescribed lower and upper thresholds. Because force magnitude is independent of the contact frame, 

αt can be obtained before estimating the local contact normal. The local normal ˆnt is estimated from recent pose, rotation relative to the initial state, and wrench history using a separately trained estimator. The estimator is trained using the same demonstrations as the task policy, with an activation-weighted cosine loss against normals obtained from registered training geometry. The proposed method is then evaluated on a real-robot drawing task using an FR3 manipulator (Franka Robotics GmbH) [36]. The evaluation considers unseen objects with different contact geometries and frictional properties to test generalization under changing physical contact conditions. Demonstrations are collected with the handheld tool, provid-ing an intuitive interface for acquiring contact-rich behavior while allowing force-domain interaction to be learned despite differences in physical realization between demonstration and robot execution. Our experiments address three research questions: 1) Can the contact factor be estimated reliably on unseen objects and surfaces? 2) Can contact factorization effectively decouple learned behavior from contact-dependent physical realization? 3) Does this factorization improve object generalization across changes in contact geometry and frictional prop-erties, and how does each component contribute?

B. Surface Estimation and Interaction Representation 

We first evaluate the adaptation module in a separate controlled-sliding experiment, distinct from the task-level evaluation in Sec IV-C. These experiments assess whether contact-dependent factors can be accurately estimated under seen and unseen surface conditions and whether the proposed interaction representation separates contact-dependent force scaling while retaining the temporal structure of measured interactions. We collect controlled sliding interactions with line contact on a small set of surface geometries and materials. The contact normal estimator is trained using paper and PTFE over flat, convex uphill, convex downhill, and concave uphill configurations, with 100 training episodes per material and configuration combination. The contact-factor estimator first computes force-based contact activation and estimates the contact normal from motion and wrench histories using a GRU. The estimated normal is then used to decompose the measured force and update the friction estimate with a scalar Kalman filter during steady sliding. Additional implementa-tion and data-collection details are provided on the project page. For evaluation, we consider both seen conditions and held-out conditions containing unseen geometries, unseen materials, or both. Cotton serves as the unseen material, and an unseen convex geometry is evaluated with paper, PTFE, and cotton. More details are available on the project page. Fig. 5 summarizes the results. Raw tangential loading varies across materials and geometries, whereas factorized residuals are centered near zero during steady sliding (a). The residuals retain phase-dependent variation, and departures from ρ = 1 outside steady sliding support learning ρ rather than fixing it to unity (b). Contact-normal errors average 

0.030 rad on seen geometries and 0.045 rad on unseen ge-ometries (c). Initialized from a condition-independent prior, friction estimates approach their reference scales during steady sliding (d). With cotton and convex B both unseen, the normal error is 0.047 rad and the steady-sliding median friction ratio is 1.20 . These results validate the contact factorization and demonstrate that the adaptation module can estimate contact factors online under unseen contact conditions (Research Question 1).

• No Force-Factorization: Inspired by the direct force prediction in ForceMimic [6], this variant represents force observations and actions in physical units rather than fac-torized coordinates. • No Learned Contact-Normal Estimation: This variant replaces the learned contact-normal estimator with an analytic estimator based on measured motion and force, while retaining the same policy and remaining compo-nents. Comparison with the full method evaluates the role of learned contact-normal estimation in transferring across varying surface geometries. Table I shows that FACE achieves 65% success (13/20) across unseen paper cup deformations, compared with 0% for the analytic contact-normal variant. On unseen eggs, the corresponding rates are 68% and 4%. Excessive-force fail-ures on eggs decrease from 56% with the analytic estimator to 8% with FACE, although insufficient-force failures remain at 24%. Visual Observation Only exhibits contact loss in 96% of egg trials, whereas no such failures are observed with FACE. No Force-Factorization achieves 45% across cup de-formations and exceeds FACE in Vertical (mild) by one trial. These results highlight the contribution of learned contact-normal estimation, although gains vary across conditions. Table II shows that FACE succeeds in 29/30 trials (96.7%) across the three unseen surface conditions, compared with 15/30 (50%) for No Force-Factorization. Of its 15 failures, 11 are excessive-force failures on PVC tape and the plastic shell, while 4 are incorrect-motion failures on the wet plastic shell. The excessive-force failures are consistent with the sensitivity of direct physical force targets to changes in contact-dependent force scale, whereas FACE’s only failure is insufficient force on the wet plastic shell. The analytic contact-normal variant succeeds in 5/30 trials, further sup-porting the role of learned contact-normal estimation in translating factorized interaction into physical commands. Visual Observation Only and Force as Observation succeed in 3/30 and 1/30 trials, respectively, indicating that neither omitting physical interaction nor simply conditioning on raw force is sufficient under surface changes. Together, these results support separating learned interac-tion from its contact-dependent physical execution to im-prove task generalization under unseen geometry and sur-face conditions (Research Question 2). The ablations high-light contributions from both force factorization and learned contact-normal estimation, while remaining insufficient-force and contact-loss failures indicate limitations in contact reg-ulation (Research Question 3). V. C ONCLUSION 

We present FACE, a contact-factorized imitation learn-ing framework that separates intended task behavior from environment-dependent contact factors. Force observations and actions are represented in normalized coordinates defined by the local contact frame and friction scale. A learned contact-normal estimator and an online friction filter enable execution to adapt to current contact conditions without updating the task policy. Using demonstrations collected with a force-sensing handheld interface, our real-robot ex-periments show improved transfer to unseen surface proper-ties and geometries. Controlled comparisons highlight the complementary roles of force factorization and contact-normal adaptation in maintaining the intended interaction. These results support separating task behavior from contact-dependent force scale and direction as a means of improving generalization in contact-rich imitation learning. As a poten-tial limitation, friction estimation remains challenging when sliding is limited. Integrating visual cues provides a concrete path toward resolving this partial observability and extending the framework to deformable and multi-contact interactions.
