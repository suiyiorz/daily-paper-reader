Abstract. Long-duration outdoor coverage with autonomous platforms remains challenging beyond classical planning: deployments face localiza-tion drift in open spaces, obstacles in cluttered sites, controller feasibility in turn-heavy maneuvers, and persistent autonomy with energy manage-ment. We propose a unified ROS 2 architecture for outdoor coverage that combines coverage planning, robust localization, and Nav2-based execution. A dual-antenna RTK-GNSS fused in an EKF keeps the robot pose, both position and heading, accurate across long missions; three controller-aware refinements are added to a mature coverage planner; a Behavior-Tree mission manager coordinates multi-goal execution, lay-ered recovery, cost-aware goal management, and autonomous docking for return-to-charge. We validate the stack through simulation and real-world trials across multiple outdoor areas with varying geometries and obstacle densities. Overall, these results show that the proposed stack can reliably complete outdoor coverage missions across varied areas, sweep-ing 93 .1 % to 96 .1 % of the planned coverage area. 

Keywords: Coverage path planning, Autonomous navigation, Behavior trees, Field robotics, ROS 2 

## 1 Introduction 

Industrial outdoor sites such as solar farms, logistics yards, and large managed green areas increasingly deploy autonomous platforms for repetitive maintenance (mowing, sweeping, inspection, soil conditioning). Coverage strategies are well studied in controlled settings, but translating them into reliable field opera-tion remains hard: deployments require stable localization in open spaces, safe interaction with both known and unknown obstacles, long-duration execution, smooth return-to-charge, and other mission-level capabilities. These constraints expose three closely related gaps in many existing ap-proaches. First, low-speed motion with frequent turns makes heading estimation difficult, especially during intermittent GNSS degradation. Second, geometri-cally optimal coverage paths may still prove fragile at execution time when way-point distribution and turn resolution do not align with controller feasibility. ## 2 Related Works 

Coverage path planning and field pipelines. Coverage path planning (CPP) has a long history in robotics, with classical methods guaranteeing complete cover-age through geometric decomposition, such as the boustrophedon cellular de-composition [2] and related grid and spanning-tree formulations [3]. Since a length-optimal complete coverage path is generally costly to compute, practical planners typically rely on cell decomposition and cover each cell with parallel swaths [4]. This decomposition-based swath-and-route strategy remains widely used in field deployments. For real vehicles, this approach is consolidated into reusable pipelines: Fields2Cover (F2C) [5] packages headland and swath gen-eration, routing, and path synthesis into a modular open-source library. The related Fields2Benchmark [6] standardizes evaluation on a large dataset of real fields with varied geometries, while reviews organize coverage into coverage plan generation, swath traversal ordering, and smooth drivable-path planning [7]. 

Coverage transitions and execution robustness. Headland and inter-swath tran-sitions are an active subproblem of their own, addressed by continuous-curvature headland coverage with clothoid turns at field corners [8] and by optimization-based motion planning for the single headland turns that arise in constrained fields carrying obstacles [9]. A complementary line targets execution-time ro-bustness: anytime replanning repairs only the affected sections of a path when initially unknown obstacles appear, within a bounded time budget [10], while online multi-robot planners distribute coverage over a fleet [11, 12]. What these pipelines leave open is how reliably a real robot executes the resulting plan, maintaining clearance between passes and handling unexpected obstacles. 

Outdoor localization and heading estimation. Outdoor coverage execution re-quires a stable global pose. During coverage, the robot often moves slowly and 4 Leonardo Gargani et al. 

turns frequently, making heading difficult to estimate from wheel odometry alone and prone to drift over long missions. In the Robot Operating System (ROS), heterogeneous sensor measurements are often fused through a configurable ex-tended Kalman filter [13]. Separately, dual-antenna RTK-GNSS can provide a motion-independent yaw measurement, which can be included in the filter to improve attitude estimation [14]. For long coverage missions, measured-yaw gat-ing and direction-of-travel fallback strategies can help maintain a usable heading estimate when the dual-antenna solution temporarily degrades. 

Execution, behavior trees, and persistent autonomy. Reliable field operation also relies on the execution layer. Nav2 [1, 15] is a widely used ROS 2 navigation substrate with Behavior-Tree (BT) orchestration, layered costmaps, controllers, and recovery behaviors. BTs are favored over finite-state machines for modular, reactive execution [16]. Persistent autonomy with charging and recovery was demonstrated in long-term indoor deployments [17], and recent work formalizes fleet-level charging schedules for long-duration autonomy [18]. These components are often treated separately; here, we combine them in a single supervisor for outdoor coverage, including recovery, goal management, and return-to-charge. We integrate these lines of work into a single outdoor-coverage stack for a field robot and validate it outdoors. The execution layer follows a deterministic route and handles unknown obstacles by temporarily deferring blocked goals to the reactive navigation layer, rather than modifying the offline plan [10]. The local-ization stack builds on the standard ROS filtering approach for heterogeneous sensors [13], adding dual-antenna yaw gating and a direction-of-travel fallback for turn-heavy coverage. Finally, because the differential-drive platform can ro-tate in place, the planning refinements focus on waypoint density and inter-swath connector clearance rather than continuous-curvature headland turns. 

## 3 System Description and Architecture 

This section defines the setting and system used in the paper. We first formalize the coverage task and admissible workspace, then present the offline-to-online architecture and hardware platform, and finally detail the localization stack that maintains accurate global pose over long, turn-heavy missions in open fields.

3.3 Localization 

Holding an accurate heading is the precondition for trackable coverage, and it is the hardest state to maintain on this platform: in tight turns, the heading inferred from wheel motion alone drifts, and over a long mission, this error accumulates until the global pose becomes unreliable. Therefore, we fuse wheel odometry with a dual-antenna RTK-GNSS module that supplies a georeferenced position pk = ( xk, y k) and a motion-independent yaw ψk. These measurements are combined in a custom 12-state EKF that runs in 2D-constrained mode on planar terrain and preserves the robot’s planar pose estimate. ## 4 Coverage Planning 

The offline stage produces the reusable coverage route in two sequential parts: it first prepares the admissible region from the site survey, then plans swaths over it with Fields2Cover and our controller-aware refinements. 

4.1 Coverage Preparation 

Preparation produces the admissible region A so that all geometry shares a single metric frame that the coverage planner and the navigation stack both consume. 

Perimeter acquisition is performed once per site or after a layout change by either driving the robot along the boundary while logging georeferenced poses or manually entering known waypoints. The resulting poses are subsampled by translation and yaw thresholds, optionally edited for consistency, and accepted only if they form a valid, closed, non-self-intersecting polygon B.

Obstacle preparation then takes the known-obstacle footprints from a 2D map, a sliced 3D model, or a projected reconstruction, and places them metri-cally with a similarity transformation from pixel-to-world correspondences. The footprints are then buffered by the safety margin δ (Sec. 3.1) and subtracted from B to yield the polygonal admissible model A used by all later stages. 8 Leonardo Gargani et al. 

4.2 Controller-aware Refinements 

We build on Fields2Cover [5] (F2C), a mature open-source geometric coverage planner. Three refinements adapt F2C’s geometrically valid output for closed-loop execution, each formalized relative to the native planner behavior it re-places. Given the admissible region A and the vehicle parameters ( w, r min ), F2C runs four deterministic stages: it (i) offsets the boundary inward to re-serve a constant-width headland for turning; (ii) fills the interior with paral-lel swaths spaced by the working width w; (iii) orders the swaths into a route 

Σ = ( σ1, σ 2, . . . ) that minimizes non-productive travel, for which it provides sev-eral strategies such as boustrophedon, snake, and spiral orderings; and (iv) syn-thesizes a drivable path γF2C by joining consecutive swaths with motion prim-itives (Dubins for forward-only motion, Reeds-Shepp when reverse is allowed). F2C is geometrically sound overall, but γF2C targets geometric validity rather than closed-loop trackability and transition clearance. 

Collinearity-Aware Resampling. F2C samples turns densely but can leave long straight swaths with few intermediate poses, which degrades tracking and overlap on the most coverage-critical segments. A uniform resampling would fix the straights but dilute the dense turn sampling that F2C provides, so we instead adapt the density to the local geometry, resampling the waypoint se-quence without altering the path it traces. Layout-Aware Obstacle Clustering. Densely packed small obstacles can create many gaps that are too narrow for the robot to traverse safely, leading F2C to generate fragile transitions. Therefore, we merge nearby obstacles using a morphological operation: the obstacle layer is rasterized into a binary occu-pancy image and dilated with a rectangular structuring element, i.e., a small rectangular mask used to expand occupied pixels. This mask is wider across the driving direction than along it and is sized according to the robot clearance. After dilation, we extract the outer contours of the expanded occupied regions to obtain the merged obstacle polygons. Two obstacles merge when the gap between them is smaller than the mask width in the connection direction, so one rasterize-dilate-contour pass both enforces the safety margin and removes sub-traversable gaps. The absorbed area is limited to those narrow gaps, so the admissible region shrinks only slightly while the merged layout keeps the gener-ated trajectories collision-free and feasible to execute in the field.

5.1 Behavior-Tree Mission Executive 

Mission execution is organized as a Behavior Tree on two levels (Fig. 4). A compact mission tree runs the deployment loop: while the battery is sufficient for operation, it extracts the route goals, undocks, hands the entire route to the navigation layer, and docks again; otherwise, low battery or a failed mission returns the robot to the charging station. The navigation layer is a Nav2 BT with two stages: a get-to-route phase that drives to the first swath using a single-goal horizon, and a recovery-wrapped plan-and-follow loop that executes the remaining route. The loop plans over a short horizon of the next K=4 goals, prunes each goal once the robot passes within its acceptance radius, truncates the controller path to a few meters ahead, and re-smooths it every control cycle, so replanning stays frequent and local regardless of the route length. Robustness comes from a recovery subtree of escalating actions (Fig. 4), tried one at a time under a bounded retry budget: clearing the local then global costmap, in-place rotation to refresh perception, a short wait for unknown ob-stacles to clear, and a short controlled back-up to free the robot when stuck. 

5.2 Reactive Obstacle Handling and Goal Management 

Obstacle avoidance separates global intent from local safety. The local costmap inflates LiDAR-observed occupancy into safety buffers that discourage passages too narrow to execute reliably, while the global costmap holds only the opera-tional boundary and the known obstacles from preparation (Sec. 4.1), so offline planning sets intent and online perception enforces safety. Unknown-obstacle handling during coverage is treated as a goal-management problem, not merely a replanning one: because coverage trajectories are densely sampled, an unknown obstacle may overlap upcoming goals, and replanning alone may still drive toward a blocked pose. 5.3 Autonomous Docking 

Long-duration operation is supported by autonomous docking integrated into the same BT supervisor, which interrupts coverage and triggers docking once the battery falls below its threshold, so return-to-charge is part of the mission logic rather than a special case handled outside it. Docking follows a two-stage procedure (Fig. 5): the robot first navigates to a predefined staging pose near the charging station, then runs a perception-guided alignment phase in which a calibrated camera detects a fiducial ArUco marker [19] and estimates its pose as the alignment target; the staging pose sits at a fixed offset ahead of the dock with a yaw that keeps the marker visible. Docking success is inferred from a change in the robot’s power state, and undocking re-runs the same alignment approach in reverse to back the robot out to the staging pose.

6.1 Experimental Setup and Test Scenarios 

The evaluation covers both phases: offline preparation (perimeter recording, ob-stacle extraction, and coverage computation; Secs. 4.1, 4.2) and online execution (route following, multi-goal navigation, recovery, docking, and unknown-obstacle avoidance; Sec. 5). The stack was first checked in simulation, including on fields larger than the outdoor test sites. Real-world trials then tested the complete pipeline under realistic sensing and terrain conditions, with each run performed autonomously from start to finish using the same operating protocol. We defined five operational areas: convex (01, 02, 05) and non-convex (03, 04), without known obstacles (01, 02) and with them (03, 04, 05). Field sizes reach about 1600 m 2. Across trials, we varied geometry, the number and size of known obstacle footprints, and the presence of unknown obstacles such as pedestrians or temporarily placed objects in the workspace. Known obstacles were handled offline by buffering their footprints out of A, whereas unknown obstacles were handled online through the costmap and goal-management mechanisms.

## 7 Conclusion and Future Work 

We presented a unified ROS 2 system for outdoor coverage with a single robot. The system combines dual-antenna RTK-GNSS localization, controller-oriented refinements to Fields2Cover, and a Nav2 Behavior Tree executive for long-duration execution with obstacle handling, recovery, and autonomous docking. Across five outdoor areas with different shapes, sizes, and obstacle layouts, the system completed every coverage route, sweeping 93 .1 % to 96 .1 % of the planned coverage area; offline planning finished in seconds, and the executive handled un-known obstacles and returned to charge without intervention. The same stack was also checked in simulation on fields larger than the outdoor sites, suggesting that the pipeline can scale beyond the areas evaluated here in practice. Fu-ture work will extend the system to multi-robot coordination and to localization methods that remain reliable when GNSS is highly degraded or unavailable.
